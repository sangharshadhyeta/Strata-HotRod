Technical note

Running a Large Mixture-of-Experts Language Model on Commodity Hardware

From the solid.cpp experiments to the Strata-HotRod inference engine

Prepared by: Sumit Sodhi, Assistant Administrative Officer, Information Systems (IS-II) Wing, Office of the Comptroller and Auditor General of India, New Delhi  ·  Date: November 2026

Legacy copy (18 Nov 2026): https://github.com/sangharshadhyeta/Strata-HotRod/tree/legacy-solid

1. Purpose and summary

This note records work carried out between August and November 2026 on running very large language models on a single consumer graphics card and an ordinary personal computer, without cloud services. The work consisted of two connected projects. solid.cpp (13 August to 3 October 2026) was an experimental fork of llama.cpp, used to establish by measurement which techniques are effective. Strata-HotRod (10 October to 15 November 2026) incorporates the techniques that proved effective into Strata, an open-source inference engine written by Niko1221 and contributors, on which this work is built.

On the test machine (NVIDIA RTX 3060 with 12 GB of video memory, Intel i5-12400F, 30 GB of RAM, NVMe solid-state drive) a 78 GB quantised model, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, ran at 50 to 70 tokens per second, against 17.2 for the unmodified engine measured on the same machine on 10 October. A token is roughly three-quarters of a word. All figures are the author's own measurements on this one machine and have not been independently reproduced.

2. Background

A mixture-of-experts model consists of many specialised sub-networks (“experts”), of which only a few are used for each word. A model can therefore be much larger than the graphics card if the most-used experts are kept on the card, the next most-used in system memory, and the remainder on the drive. Two questions then decide the speed: which experts should be kept where, and what to do when an expert that is needed is not on the card.

hot: on the card (12 GB, 254 GB/s) warm: in RAM (30 GB, 24.5 GB/s over PCIe) cold: on the SSD (up to 111 GB, 3.55 GB/s)

4. Findings in solid.cpp and their implementation in HotRod

4.1 Stand-in experts

Finding

When a required expert is not on the card, the engine may wait for it or run a similar expert that is already there (a “stand-in”). On Qwen3.8-Flash-Next (87 GB) on the 12 GB card, with the same program and model, stand-ins were the largest single gain:

Expert cache off3.7
Cache on, no stand-ins8.3
Cache on, with stand-ins12.85
Model that fits (Gemma-4 26B)72–78

Pushed too far, stand-ins damage the text: at the most aggressive setting the output degenerated into meaningless words at 11.95 tokens per second, so speed alone cannot be the criterion. Three of solid.cpp’s own automatic checks proved wrong and were corrected:

CheckWhat it measuredOutcome
ReproducibilityIdentical answers across five repeated runs; the cache changes between requests, so wording varies.Fired nine times, always at 2 of 5 (sampling noise). Removed.
Fidelity scoreSimilarity to an answer without stand-ins; a stand-in differs by design.Rated a correct answer at zero. Demoted to a report.
DegeneracyRepeated 4-grams; one word dominating.Caught the only genuine memory defect. Retained.

Implementation in HotRod

A missing expert is replaced only when router weight × logit gap ≤ budget. The budget tunes itself by alternating runs over the same text, each request limits itself if re-checks show changed tokens, and the few fragile tokens are recomputed exactly. The cut-off was fixed by measurement and by reading the answers (short answers, warm, tokens per second):

4.2 Looking ahead

Finding

solid.cpp trained a small predictor on its own traffic and tried three uses of its guesses (tokens per second):

Use of the guessGemma-4 26BQwen3.8-Flash-Next
Trained, not used—9.44
Fetch experts before they are asked for56.59 (worse)9.45 (no gain)
Protect likely experts from eviction71.419.86
Eviction protection, and prefer wanted experts as stand-ins52.81 (worse)10.69

A router look-ahead that runs as part of the layer arrives exactly one layer ahead, every time; the trained predictor, queued on a worker, sometimes arrived late or not at all. Biasing the router toward experts already on the card raised the cache hit rate from 0.847 to 0.917 (+3.9% speed). A predictor trained live on a processor thread, in the earlier experiments, lost outright.

Implementation in HotRod

A learned look-ahead runs on the graphics card. It starts from the model’s own router and learns a small correction one, two and four layers ahead, so that its guess is never worse than the router’s. Reads it starts use two input/output threads, next layer first (only about 3,000 of 25,000 experts read ahead had been used, and eight threads’ wasted reads had queued ahead of the real ones). Measured: +8% code, +15% reasoning, +8% prose; drive waiting time per answer fell from 3.8–4.7 s to 2.8–3.0 s. For DeepSeek, where a missing expert is computed on the processor, the same guesses fill spare card slots one or two layers ahead (a “VRAM ring”), used only if the copy has arrived.

4.3 Memory and reads

Finding

Two defects in solid.cpp itself had been attributed to the hardware: the loader locked memory-mapped experts in memory on a model larger than RAM (turning 18.4 GiB into copies that could not be released), and every page fault read 4 MiB for a 0.88 MiB expert slice. Correcting both raised the same model on the same card from 2.81 to 12.85 tokens per second. Settings chosen at short context also failed at long context:

solid.cpp’s furthest reach was GLM-5.3-Flash (93 GB, 320 billion parameters) on 42 GB of combined memory, against the roughly 102 GB its publisher gives as a practical minimum, at 1.18 tokens per second and a 38.7% cache hit rate.

Implementation in HotRod

Pinning the hottest experts in memory had lost in solid.cpp because pinned pages cannot be reclaimed. HotRod locks its RAM tier in segments and releases them when memory is needed (+9% on the test PC). The three data links (drive to RAM, RAM to card, drive to card) are budgeted like capacities, and every step reports bytes moved per link per token. Long prompts are read one layer at a time, so each layer’s experts are read from the drive once rather than once per piece of the prompt: an 8,554-token prompt was read in 16.9 s, and 262K tokens of context fit. From 64K tokens the attention’s key/value data streams from RAM, and a long conversation’s saved state reloads in about 0.4 s (45K tokens).

4.4 Method

Finding

Each of the following was a separate before-and-after measurement on the same hardware:

Change in solid.cppEffect
Frozen expert-cache defect corrected+65%
Cache fed by prediction instead of history+41%
Overlapped uploads during prompt processing+36.0%
Expert cache against naive processor offload+31.3%
Multi-token speculative decoding+28.1%
Empirical placement search+21.6%
Prefetch double-buffer+12.7%
Real placement optimum+10.0%

Defaults were chosen by paired runs, three rounds each, with a control alongside (Gemma-4): neuron subsetting off unless calibrated on (+50%, 24 of 24 rounds); draft depth 4 (+22%, 22 of 24); draft confidence gate 0.5 (+13%, 19 of 24); probabilistic draft acceptance on (off is −9%, 20 of 24). Wrong decisions came from the order of the tests: the draft was eliminated before it was configured; a prefill figure (42.3 against 48.5 tokens/s) switched prefetch off for every decode stage; the acceptance mode was chosen after the depth it determines. Eight faults were found along the way. A “+21% to +50%” gain from three single runs was retracted as noise.

Implementation in HotRod

Every decision in the specification (SPEC.md) remains “untested” until a row in its test ledger holds a measurement on the target machine; a decision whose test fails is reopened. Comparisons use alternated paired runs with a control, every answer is read by a person, and a gain is retracted in writing if a repeat shows noise. Several October changes came from systematic code review (hangs on error paths, a deadlock in image jobs, a batch-state defect in the CUDA decode path, a read past a tensor’s storage).

6. Limitations

  • One machine, one graphics card, one drive. AMD, Intel and Apple paths exist in Strata and were not tested here.
  • Speeds are for short answers unless stated; answers were read by a person and not scored on a standard accuracy benchmark.
  • The 111 GB and DeepSeek results rest on fewer runs than the 78 GB result.
  • Figures have not been independently reproduced. The repository holds changed files and notes, not a complete buildable copy.
